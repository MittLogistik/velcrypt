Crossing a border is one of the few times a stranger may ask for your unlocked phone, with real consequences if you refuse. This guide gives a general picture, a practical checklist for iPhone, and an honest view of where tools like a decoy vault help and where they don’t. It is not legal advice: rules change and depend on where you are going and who you are.

## What the rules generally look like

A border search is an inspection of you and your belongings when entering or leaving a country. In many countries, the law gives border agencies wider powers than police normally have, sometimes without the suspicion or warrant that would be needed elsewhere.

Device searches typically fall into two kinds:

- **Manual inspection:** an officer scrolls through your unlocked phone, looking at photos, messages and apps.
- **Forensic examination:** the device is connected to specialist equipment that copies and analyses data. This is usually less common and may require more justification.

What happens if you decline to unlock also varies. Citizens generally can’t be refused entry to their own country but may have their device held. Visitors may be refused entry. Some countries treat refusing to provide a password as an offence in specific circumstances.

Because the details vary so much, check the official government guidance for each country on your route, and if you are a journalist, lawyer, activist or otherwise at higher risk, speak to a lawyer or a press-freedom or digital-rights organisation before you travel. [Journalists protecting sources](id:journalists-protecting-sources) covers that situation.

## A preparation checklist

### Before you leave

- **Carry less.** The single most effective step. If you don’t need something on the trip, move it off the phone. A search can’t expose what isn’t there.
- **Check what syncs back.** Data you remove from the phone can reappear from iCloud. Review iCloud Photos, Messages in iCloud and other sync before and during travel.
- **Sign out of what you won’t use.** Email accounts, work tools, social apps. Fewer logged-in apps means less to look through.
- **Use a strong passcode.** At least six digits, or an alphanumeric code (Settings › Face ID & Passcode › Change Passcode › Passcode Options). The [pattern vs PIN vs Face ID guide](id:pattern-lock-vs-pin-vs-face-id) explains why length matters.
- **Update iOS.** Current software has the latest security fixes.
- **Write down important contacts on paper,** including a lawyer or your embassy, in case your phone is held.

### Just before the border

- **Disable biometrics.** On iPhone 8 and later, press and hold the side button and either volume button until the power-off/SOS screen appears, then tap Cancel. Face ID and Touch ID stay disabled until you enter your passcode. (Depending on your settings, pressing the side button repeatedly can trigger Emergency SOS and call emergency services, so learn the exact gesture beforehand.)
- **Or power off completely.** After a restart, an iPhone needs the passcode, not biometrics, and much of its data stays in its most strongly protected state until that first unlock.
- **Close sensitive apps.** Don’t walk up with a vault, chat or document open on screen.

### At the inspection

- **Stay calm and polite.** Ask what is happening and why, and whether you are required to comply.
- **Don’t lie.** Making false statements to officials can turn a routine inspection into a serious problem.
- **Note details.** Officer names or badge numbers, what was inspected, how long the device was out of your sight.
- **Ask for a receipt** if your device is kept.

### After you arrive

- **Change passwords** if your phone was taken out of your sight or connected to equipment.
- **Restore data** you deliberately left behind, via a secure connection.

## How a decoy vault fits, and where it doesn’t

A [decoy vault](id:what-is-a-decoy-vault) is a second vault that opens with a different secret and holds harmless content. On a trip, it is genuinely useful against everyday risks: a fellow traveller who picks up your phone, someone at a hostel, a pickpocket who watched your passcode, or a partner scrolling at the airport. [Travelling with private photos](id:travelling-with-private-photos) has more on these situations.

With officials, be more careful:

- **Misleading officials can be an offence.** If you are asked directly whether the app contains other data, don’t answer falsely. Know whether you are required to answer at all, and ask for advice.
- **Forensic analysis may show more data exists.** Plausible deniability means nothing in the app proves that other vaults exist, but storage size and expert examination can show that the app holds encrypted data. [Plausible deniability explained](id:plausible-deniability-explained) covers this in detail.
- **Leaving data at home beats hiding it.** If a search of specific content could seriously harm you, the strongest option is not carrying it.

## Ethics and honest limits

Privacy at the border is a legitimate concern. Most people simply don’t want a stranger reading their messages to their family or looking at personal photos. Protecting that is reasonable. But privacy tools are not a way around the law, and nothing in this guide is meant to help anyone smuggle illegal material or deceive authorities. Know your rights, comply with lawful requests, and use legal advice to understand where the lines are in your situation.
