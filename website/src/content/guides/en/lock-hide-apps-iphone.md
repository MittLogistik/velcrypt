People usually search for this when they want to hand their phone to someone without worrying about what they might open: a chat app, a dating app, banking, or Photos. Since iOS 18, iPhone has a built-in way to do exactly that. It is quick and useful, but it has clear limits, and it doesn’t do one thing many people expect: lock a folder on the Home Screen.

## Lock an app with Require Face ID

Locking puts Face ID, Touch ID or your passcode in front of an app every time it opens.

1. On the Home Screen or in App Library, touch and hold the app icon until the quick actions menu opens.
2. Tap **Require Face ID** (on other models it says Touch ID or Passcode).
3. Tap **Require Face ID** again and authenticate.

Opening a locked app doesn’t unlock it for good. It locks again as soon as you quit it. To remove the lock, touch and hold the icon and choose **Don’t Require Face ID**.

According to Apple, information from a locked app also stays out of other places: notification previews, search, Siri suggestions, call history and CarPlay. A locked messaging app still tells you a message has arrived, but not what it says.

## Hide an app: the Hidden folder

Hiding goes one step further. The app disappears from the Home Screen and moves to a **Hidden** folder at the bottom of App Library.

1. Touch and hold the app icon and tap **Require Face ID**.
2. Tap **Hide and Require Face ID**, authenticate, then tap **Hide App**.
3. To open it later, swipe left past your last Home Screen page, tap the **Hidden** folder at the bottom of App Library and authenticate.

A hidden app is also locked, and it no longer appears in search results. Two rules decide what you can hide: only apps you downloaded from the App Store, not the ones that came with the iPhone, and not the app you set as your default browser.

Hiding is not invisibility. The Hidden folder is always shown in App Library, so anyone who looks can see that you have hidden apps, just not which ones. Apple also notes that names of hidden apps can appear in **Settings › Apps › Hidden Apps** (behind authentication), in **Settings › Screen Time**, in the battery settings and in your App Store purchase history. Hidden apps also stop showing notifications, so don’t hide an app whose alerts you rely on. <!-- TODO verify -->

## What app locking protects, and what it doesn’t

App locking is designed for one situation: someone is holding your unlocked iPhone for a moment. In that situation it works well. Outside it, the gaps are predictable.

- **Your passcode opens everything.** Locked and hidden apps accept the device passcode as well as Face ID. A partner, parent or anyone else who knows your passcode can open them, and can also turn the lock off.
- **Data lives in more than one place.** Locking Photos doesn’t stop other apps that already have access to your photo library. A locked chat app doesn’t protect pictures you saved from it to Photos, or files you exported to the Files app. [Which apps can see your photos](id:which-apps-can-see-your-photos-iphone) explains how to check those permissions.
- **Other devices are separate.** The lock doesn’t sync. The same app on your iPad or Mac, or the same content on iCloud.com, stays open unless you protect it there too.
- **A locked app is still visible.** Unless it is hidden, its icon stays on the Home Screen, which in itself can tell someone what you use.
- **Not everything can be locked.** Apple lists Calculator, Camera, Clock, Contacts, Find My, Maps, Shortcuts and Settings among the built-in apps that can’t be locked.

## Can you lock a folder on the iPhone Home Screen?

No. A Home Screen folder is just a way to group icons; iOS has no setting to put a password or Face ID on it, and no hidden option that adds one. There are three practical workarounds:

1. **Lock each app in the folder.** Touch and hold each icon inside the folder and choose Require Face ID. The folder stays visible, but every app in it asks for authentication.
2. **Use the Hidden folder instead.** For App Store apps, Hide and Require Face ID moves them into the Hidden folder in App Library. That is the one app folder iOS locks, although you can’t rename it or create a second one.
3. **Lock the content, not the folder.** If what you really want to protect is documents, the Files app has no folder lock either; see [how to password protect a folder on iPhone](id:password-protect-folder-iphone). For pictures, see [how to lock photos on iPhone](id:lock-photos-iphone).

Be careful with App Store apps that promise to lock other apps or folders. Some use Apple’s Screen Time framework to block apps, which behaves more like the Screen Time method below than a real lock, and none of them can encrypt another app’s data. What a third-party app can genuinely offer is a vault that stores content inside itself.

## The older method: Screen Time limits

Before iOS 18, the usual workaround was Screen Time. You set a tiny daily limit, such as one minute, for an app, turn on **Block at End of Limit** and protect the settings with a Screen Time passcode. Once the minute is used up, the app shows a time-limit screen until someone enters that passcode. In iOS 27, Apple reorganized these limits into Screen Time Schedules and Time Allowances, so the menu names differ by version. <!-- TODO verify iOS label -->

The one advantage over app locking is that the Screen Time passcode can differ from your device passcode. The drawbacks are bigger: the app still opens for the allowed minute each day, the icon stays visible, and the setup is built for parental controls rather than privacy. For most adults, Require Face ID has replaced it. For a child borrowing your phone, see [sharing your iPhone with kids](id:sharing-phone-with-kids).

## App locking is not encryption

It helps to separate two things iOS does. Your data is encrypted by iOS Data Protection, tied to your passcode, and protected while the iPhone is locked. App locking happens after that: on an unlocked phone, it decides whether a particular app may open. It doesn’t add a second key or encrypt anything again.

That is why the passcode works as a fallback, and why app locking can’t protect you from someone who knows the passcode, or who makes you unlock the phone. An encrypted vault works differently: the content inside is encrypted under a secret of its own, separate from the device passcode, so opening the phone does not open the vault.

There is no Face ID by design, and the pattern has nothing to do with your iPhone passcode, so someone who knows the passcode still can’t open your vaults.
